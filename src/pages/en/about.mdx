## Slash is a capability life wakes up

To many people, “slash” sounds chill.

To some, it means a colorful life.

To me, it’s closer to a capability life wakes up in you. It takes enough trials to wake it.

You might be thinking: trials, in your thirties?

Let me ask back: why did three friends my age die before they turned thirty? Why did friends I thought I’d build a lifetime’s work with go separate ways? Why did pets I’d had for barely three to five years leave so suddenly? In those same years, I also welcomed my first and second child.

Trials aren’t measured in years. They’re measured in density.

Reflecting on life stopped being something I wanted to do. It became something I had to do.

Later, I wrote this line in my own timeout notes:

> Real life is unpredictable—it could be long, or end in the next heartbeat. Prepare for longevity and approach each day as if it could be your last.

Fatherhood is a role that turns you into a slash person whether you planned it or not.

Almost everything can be redesigned. Your child’s childhood can’t.

## Where it came from

<Image src={PhasesImage} alt="KL’s four life phases: ages 0–9, 10–18, 19–27, and 28 to now" />

Looking back, I’ve lived this way since I was a kid.

**Ages 0–9.** I was born into a business family, loved, and free to grow. Play, I learned, can be free.

**Ages 10–18.** In elementary school, the family business slowed and the family took on debt. I started working the family stall after school and kept working while studying until I finished high school. What I learned wasn’t “how to be busy.” It was how to stay free inside responsibilities I couldn’t quit.

**Ages 19–27.** In college, I kept up with coursework while throwing myself into student council, clubs, and camps. There were academic highs, and a stretch close to homelessness. I got married and was baptized.

In my first five years out of college, I worked nearly 100 hours a week to turn my family’s situation around. I thought if I held on a little longer, I’d be free. Instead, my life fell apart, sleep ran out, and I had three car accidents in a row.

Holding on is not a system.

**Age 28 to now.** I stopped and spent one to two years resetting. Then I became a father and started my own business. As responsibilities grew, I optimized, systematized, and automated my life piece by piece. Over the past three years, deep work with AI is what finally brought my work week down to about 20 hours and gave the time back to health, family, and myself.

So these days feel like reliving my childhood.

## Who I am now

I graduated in Biomedical Engineering from National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University. I’ve spent 7 years in health tech (sports tech, health screening and preventive medicine, medical platforms) as a PM, BD, and SWE. I also spent 4 years as a learning coach and curriculum developer for coding courses, ran a morning reading club for two and a half years, and led 200+ small-group discussions.

Since the reset, a typical week looks like this:

- **Work:** about 20 hours, roughly half a standard full-time week.
- **Kids:** 6–9 hours a day with them as they grow.
- **Exercise:** twice a week, averaging more than 150 minutes of high-intensity activity.
- **Sleep:** three full nights a week, averaging over 8 hours.

I didn’t spend the time I won back on more work. I gave it back to what matters most.

## What I mean by “born free”

Once the slash woke up, it didn’t make me busier. It gave me a right: to be born free, and to make the whole journey rich.

That freedom doesn’t mean doing whatever I want.

In _Finite and Infinite Games_, James Carse separates two kinds of games: a finite game is played to win, an infinite game is played to keep the play going. Whoever must play, cannot play.

For me, freedom comes down to four things:

- **Awareness:** see which game you’re actually playing.
- **Choice:** set your own direction instead of living by the script society hands you.
- **The long game:** play to keep playing, not just to win once for now.
- **Enjoyment:** the whole journey is full of fun, not just a satisfying finish line.

So I refuse the default script: trade health for wealth, take on endless obligations to get ahead, and give up living for yourself to live for others. It looks stable, but the regret often surfaces only years later.

If I write the opening quote the way an engineer would, it fits on one line:

```ts
Love(time, space) = Infinity;
```
